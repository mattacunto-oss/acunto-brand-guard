Executive Brand Monitoring – Business Sketch

Prepared for Matthew Acunto · Draft, Oct 6, 2026 · Internal working document

1. One-sentence pitch

A subscription service that continuously watches the public internet for threats to an athlete's, team's, or high-net-worth individual's name, image, and information (impersonators, deepfakes, leaks, fake merch, lookalike domains, and pile-ons) and alerts the subscriber with a clear severity rating and a recommended next step.

2. Who pays and why

  • NIL athletes and their agents: an athlete's name and image now earn money directly. Impersonator accounts, fake endorsements, and counterfeit merch take income and damage trust with sponsors. Agents want proof they're protecting the asset.
  • College athletic departments: they have a duty of care to many athletes, many of them 18–22 and some minors. They also face public-records, compliance, and reputational exposure, and need early warning on harassment, doxxing, and scams aimed at their roster.
  • Pro teams and leagues: protecting the team brand, player and executive impersonation, ticket and merch scams, and leaked internal material appearing publicly.
  • HNWI and family offices: executive impersonation (fraud and phishing aimed at staff), exposure of addresses and family details, and reputation attacks. They want quiet, discreet, white-glove handling.
  • Talent agencies: one contract covers a whole client roster. Monitoring becomes a value-add service the agency can resell or bundle.

Core buying reason: they find out early, before a sponsor, reporter, or fan finds it first, and they get a ready next step instead of a raw feed of mentions.

3. What we watch (public web only)

  • News and blogs mentioning the subject
  • Public social media mentions, hashtags, and trends
  • Deepfakes and impersonation accounts (fake profiles, AI-generated video, audio, and images using the subject's likeness)
  • Leaked documents that appear on publicly accessible paste and leak sites
  • Fake merch and storefronts (marketplaces, print-on-demand, social shops)
  • Phishing and lookalike domains (newly registered domains resembling the subject's name or brand)
  • Doxxing attempts that have already been posted publicly (home address, phone, family details)
  • Review and reputation attacks (coordinated negative reviews, smear campaigns)

Scope boundary (state this explicitly in every contract and sales sheet): the service monitors publicly available information plus keywords, handles, and images the subscriber provides. It does NOT involve hacking, accessing or scraping private accounts, buying stolen or breached data, logging into closed forums under false pretenses, or surveilling anyone's devices or communications.

4. Product

  • Subscriber dashboard: watchlist, live hits, status (new, reviewing, actioned, closed), and history.
  • Alert channels: email, SMS, and Slack. The subscriber picks which severity goes where.
  • Severity triage: Critical (doxxing, credible threat, active impersonation scam, leaked private docs), High (deepfake, fake store, lookalike domain), Medium (reputation pile-on), Low/Info (general mentions).
  • Weekly digest: a one-page summary of trends, closed items, and open risks.
  • Optional takedown playbooks: step-by-step guides for platform reporting tools, trademark and copyright notices, and registrar abuse reports, plus introductions to partner attorneys and takedown firms. We refer out. We don't do do-it-yourself removals that cross legal lines.

5. How "hunting" works in practice

  1. Watchlists: at onboarding, the subscriber authorizes names, nicknames, handles, jersey numbers, brand marks, family names they want covered, addresses to watch for exposure, and reference photos.
  2. Name and face search on public sources: keyword and alert tools, social platform search and official APIs, reverse image search, and checks of public marketplaces and domain registration feeds. Facial matching is done only against the subscriber's own authorized photos, only on public content, and in compliance with biometric laws such as Illinois BIPA.
  3. Brand keywords: trademarks, slogans, logos, and combinations like "[name] + giveaway / crypto / merch / leaked."
  4. AI summarization: hits are de-duplicated, clustered, summarized, and given a draft severity score.
  5. Human review: an analyst confirms every High or Critical alert before it is sent, to cut false alarms and avoid panicking clients.

6. Pricing sketch (all figures are estimates to test, not validated)

  • NIL individual: about $50–$250/month (self-serve dashboard plus alerts; higher tiers add human review and the digest)
  • Team / department package: about $1,500–$7,500/month depending on roster size (for example, one sport or a full department), plus an onboarding fee
  • HNWI / family office retainer: about $2,000–$10,000+/month, covering principal plus family, white-glove analyst access, and incident support
  • Add-ons: incident response hours, takedown coordination (passed through to partners), and quarterly exposure reports

7. Legal and ethics guardrails

Not legal advice. Have a qualified attorney (privacy, sports and NCAA compliance, and contracts) review this before launch.

  • Public data only: no unauthorized access to any system or account (consider the CFAA and state equivalents).
  • Subscriber authorization: monitor only people who have consented, or whom the subscriber is legally authorized to cover. Never monitor third parties like ex-partners or rivals on request.
  • Respect platform terms of service: prefer official APIs and licensed data. Scraping rules vary and change.
  • Minors in NIL: require parent or guardian consent. Follow school and conference rules and FERPA when working with institutions. Keep data on minors to a minimum.
  • No "record erasing": never promise to remove criminal records, news articles, or lawful content illegally. Use only legitimate channels (platform policies, DMCA and trademark notices, legal counsel).
  • Liability for false alerts: clear terms of service and limitations of liability, human review on high-severity alerts, errors-and-omissions and cyber insurance, and careful wording ("possible impersonation," not accusations).
  • Data security: watchlists are themselves sensitive (addresses, family details). Encrypt, limit access, and keep audit logs.
  • Privacy and biometrics: check state privacy laws and biometric rules before using facial matching.

8. Competitive landscape

This sits where several existing categories overlap. Brand protection vendors (e.g., Red Points, Corsearch, BrandShield) focus on counterfeits and IP takedowns for companies. Digital risk protection and executive protection platforms (e.g., ZeroFox, Doppel, BlackCloak) cover impersonation, phishing, and executive exposure, mostly sold to enterprises. Personal data removal services (e.g., DeleteMe, Kanary) clear data-broker listings. Social listening tools (e.g., Brandwatch, Meltwater) track mentions for marketing teams. Athlete-focused tools (e.g., Opendorse and Teamworks/INFLCR for NIL and content, plus online-abuse monitoring firms such as Signify) serve sports organizations. The opening: a person-centric, sports-native, analyst-reviewed service priced for individuals and athletic departments, which sits between expensive enterprise digital risk protection platforms and do-it-yourself Google Alerts. Competitors and pricing should be verified during discovery.

9. 30-day MVP

  • Week 1: recruit 5 pilot watchlists (for example, 2 NIL athletes, 1 college program or team, 1 pro athlete or agency client, 1 HNWI) on free or discounted pilots with signed authorization and scope letters.
  • Weeks 1–2: build a manual and semi-automated stack from alert tools, platform search, reverse image search, lookalike-domain feeds, marketplace searches, and a spreadsheet or Airtable for triage. Add AI summarization of daily hits.
  • Weeks 2–4: send alert emails by severity plus a weekly digest. Track time per hit, true and false positive rates, and client reactions.
  • Deliverable: a one-page sales sheet (problem, what we watch, scope boundary, sample redacted alert, pricing tiers) and 2–3 pilot testimonials or case notes.
  • Go/no-go signal: at least 2 pilots willing to pay, and a meaningful finding (impersonation, fake store, lookalike domain, etc.) for most watchlists.
